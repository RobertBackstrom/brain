01 The offer

A week on-site, the stack running in your actual project.

A studio invites a couple of Elias developers to its office for a week. We sit next to their audio and tech team and integrate the Elias stack into their project. No salesperson in the room - just developers solving the integration together.

What the studio gets out of the week:

  • Elias running in their actual project, not in a sandbox.
  • The stack wired into their engine - Unity, Godot and Unreal out of the box, and proprietary in-house engines via the Python and C/C++ API. Both our deepest integrations, Elemental and Fatshark, are on proprietary engines.
  • Their sound designers testing the new workflow live on their own content - visual scripting, real-time editing, no soundbank builds.
  • A technical walkthrough of what it takes to carry it all the way to release.
  • Their team learns the stack from us while we build, so they are not left on their own after the week.

What we need from the studio: a desk for 1-2 Elias developers for the week, access to a build and their audio pipeline, and their audio and tech people available to work alongside us - not just a kickoff.

Format: a week on-site is the default. It can be shortened to a few days for smaller scope, or run partly remote (the Agate model) when travel is not an option.

02 Why it works

Middleware switches die on one question. We answer it by showing.

Almost every middleware switch stalls on the same thing: "will it work in our pipeline?" No demo in the world answers that. A hack week does - by not answering, but showing.

And it works where off-the-shelf middleware can't. Our two deepest integrations - Elemental and Fatshark's proprietary Stingray-based engine - are both on in-house engines, done hands-on in exactly this format. Developers on-site plus the Python and C/C++ API is what plugs Elias into tech a plug-and-play middleware never reaches.

We don't sell, we integrate

The studio sees the stack running in their own game before they sign anything. The risk moves from them to us.

Dev-to-dev builds trust faster

Their programmers trust our programmers in a way they never trust a sales deck.

We learn the needs by working

The whole motion is about Elias developers getting to sit down with the audio team and understand what the needs actually are. The week gives us that, and it makes the next version better.

A natural path to a licence

A good week ends with the stack in the build and a team that already knows the tool. The step to a licence is a small one.

What the week looks like

Two Elias developers, their audio team, a build and a week. We wire in the stack, their designers start building sound in it, we clear friction in real time.

Monday: the stack running against their engine. Friday: sound running live in their actual game, and a team that knows the tool.

03 The case - Fatshark

A studio with a heavy audio history let us come in and work inside the pipeline.

The relationship. Robert Bäckström, running Elias biz-dev, co-founded Fatshark, so the way in was warm from the start. Fatshark is also close to Elias at the ownership level - both sit under KM Troedsson. The need has been there a long time: back in the Darktide production Fatshark wrestled with the audio side - no dedicated audio programmer, no audio director, designers loading in all the sound all the time and hitting the memory ceiling. Classic middleware pain.

What we did in 2026. Elias and Fatshark met at Fatshark Studios on 9 April 2026, with Mikael Hansson on the Fatshark side. Erik Brattlöf (Tech Audio & Product Owner at Elias) set up a shared Slack channel straight after and led the integration from the Elias side. The Elias team on-site was Erik, Anton and Andreas, working directly against Fatshark's build and audio pipeline. The integration ran against Fatshark's proprietary Stingray-based in-house engine, not an off-the-shelf Unity or Unreal project - the hard case, and the one that proves the most.

Why the case carries. It is not a logo on a slide - it is a studio with a known, hard audio history that let us come in and work inside their pipeline. Fatshark is now an active integration partner and a strong referral source into the Swedish studio scene. Exactly the objection the hack week is built to solve.

07 How we pitch it

The hook, who it fits, and what we ask for.

  • The hook. "We come in for a week and integrate the stack into your game, side by side with your audio team. By the end of the week it runs in your build."
  • Primary CTA. A referral ask, not a demo ask: "Who on your side is the right person to talk integration with?" - dramatically easier to say yes to.
  • Who it fits. Studios in pre-production or early production with no hard-locked middleware - on any engine, including proprietary in-house tech, which is exactly where the on-site week earns its keep. Smaller, ambitious teams where a fresh project means free audio choices.
  • Positioning. State-of-the-art audio tech - real-time raytraced acoustics, GPU spatial audio, post-Metasound - not a music tool.

Proof-point rule - read before anything goes to a prospect

  • No logo-drops in prospect material. The old names (Arrowhead, Avalanche, Iron Gate, Hazelight, Fatshark) were users of the old Elias (the music tool) and must not be claimed as proof-points for the new middleware externally.
  • Elemental and Agate are not named in drafts. They are tracked in the wiki but not yet nameworthy enough to anchor a pitch.
  • External defaults: tech framing (real-time raytraced acoustics, GPU spatial audio, post-Metasound) + investor framing (KM Troedsson, Linus Blomberg) for Swedish peer targets.
  • External variant: run the case anonymised ("a Swedish action studio with a heavy audio history let us come in for a week and integrate inside their pipeline"). The names stay in this internal document.
