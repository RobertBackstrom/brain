02The market

You have picked the right shelf

The most capital efficient genre on Steam right now is called friendslop. Short rounds, low price, built to be clipped and shared. House Siege has the one property that actually decides outcomes there: a hook that works on a muted autoplaying video with no explanation.

03The scale

One decision determines whether this is buildable

House Siege as your material describes it, with 4v4 or 5v5, full structural physics, six house archetypes, progression, matchmaking, backend, cosmetics and a console roadmap, is a 15 to 25 million krona project over roughly three years. That is not a criticism of the idea. It is what that level of ambition costs, whoever builds it.

The same game scaled to 3v3, one house, chunk based destruction and six minute rounds reaches a playable beta in nine months for under three million.

Scaling down is not lowering the ambition. It is putting the evidence in front of the money.

04Core loop

The house is its own building material

Your material asks exactly the right question: is building and repairing as much fun as destroying? We went looking for a game that answers yes, and we cannot find one. Teardown, Brick Rigs, Besiege and Instruments of Destruction are all pure destruction. Nobody has managed to make repair the fun half.

So we propose that the defender's verb becomes destructive too.

Defenders have no build resources. They get materials by tearing the house apart from the inside. You need to board the front door, there is no pile of planks, there is a staircase. You rip out the staircase, drag it up and slam it against the door.

Furniture is free. When the furniture runs out you start eating load bearing structure, and your own Integrity drops.

That does three things at once. Defending becomes as physical and as funny as attacking, with nobody standing still holding a button. Every defence costs something, so you are digging into your own meter to survive the next minute. And the map is reshaped by the defence, not only by the attack: you tore out the staircase, now you cannot get upstairs either.

The loop, fifteen to twenty five seconds

GRAB
Rip something loose. A door, a bathtub, a fridge, a load bearing beam.
HAUL
Carry it. Heavy, awkward, you are slow and you look stupid.
SLAM
Put it down hard. Defenders board a gap. Attackers use the same object as a ram or a ramp.
BREAK
Someone destroys what you just placed. The loop starts again.

Both teams use the same four verbs. That is a design choice, but it is also a budget choice: one interaction model instead of two halves the cost of the prototype and makes balance easier to hold over time.

05The risks

What the plan has to answer

These five things decide whether House Siege becomes a game. We put them up front, because the whole plan below is built to answer them in the right order and at the lowest possible cost.

Defending has to be fun
The biggest risk, and the one we have put a concrete proposal against above. The question gets answered in month two of step two, not month six. If the answer is no, the project should stop there.
Destruction plus multiplayer
The most expensive combination there is at this project size. Teardown has no multiplayer and that is no accident. The answer is to synchronise damage events rather than physics state, meaning we only send which pieces broke and let the debris fly slightly differently for each player. That is standard practice and it makes the problem tractable.
PvP needs people
A game for six players needs six players. That cold start problem is what sinks most smaller PvP titles. This is why bots are not an extra feature in this plan. They are part of the product and they get their own budgeted milestone.
Balance is a running cost
Asymmetric games need tuning for years. Rainbow Six Siege has been at it for a decade. That argues for fewer tools and clearer roles from the start, and for switching sides every round so imbalance hits both teams equally.
Price decides the game
Your material describes a premium title. The genre House Siege fits sits at six to eight dollars. Those are two different products with two different budgets, and the choice needs making early because everything else follows from it. We recommend the lower price and the larger audience.
06Art direction

Flatpack

The house should visibly be built from parts that click together, like flat pack furniture. That makes destruction readable, because you can see exactly which piece came loose, and it makes production cheap, because one kit covers six houses.

The art should look like a person made it quickly, not like a machine made it perfectly.

That is a commercial position rather than an aesthetic one. This audience recognises over polished marketing material instantly and reads it as a sign that there is no game behind it. Rough but coherent reads the opposite way: somebody is actually building something and is in on the joke. Every one of the best selling titles in the tables above looks deliberately simple.

References, and what we take from each

Colour instead of detail

A piece goes from intact to cracked to gone through colour, not texture detail. Readable at small sizes and dramatically cheaper to produce.

One costume slot per character

Blank figures with one swappable layer. That opens a route to cosmetics later without building character art now.

Fixed wide camera

You need to see the whole house fall. Clippability beats immersion, because the clip is the marketing.

Images above are the reference titles on Steam, shown to fix the target. They are not House Siege artwork. Concept art in this direction is the first deliverable of step one, and we would produce it as real renders from the actual build rather than as generated images, for the reason in the pull quote.

07Step one

The store page, before the game

The goal is a published Steam page, a trailer that looks like gameplay, and around thirty vertical clips for TikTok, Reels and Shorts. Without building the game.

The method is a trailer build. Collapses are authored as baked simulations and played back as animation, so we need no destruction engine in this step. The house is built on a purchased modular kit that we give its own identity through a custom shader, a locked palette and an original character silhouette. That is the part that stops the material looking bought, and it is the one part we do not cut.

08The gate

The number that decides step two

The entire point of building the store page first is that it produces a number which makes the next decision easy. We suggest you commit to the number in advance, before you know the outcome.

The benchmark comes from outside. Polden Publishing is a publisher specialising in exactly this genre and working from data: they run a marketing test before signing anything, and their published threshold for signing a game is 30,000 wishlists from that test. They state 150,000 dollars as their average investment per title and report more than a million wishlists generated before production decisions across their portfolio.

One important nuance. 30,000 wishlists is a threshold for continuing to invest, not for making money. At normal thirty day conversion it corresponds to roughly 11,400 copies sold, which pays for nothing. That number means the concept carries. For the whole plan to work we want to see around 150,000 wishlists at launch, which is entirely normal in this genre if step two delivers a beta people want to clip.

09Step two

The prototype, and the road to Steam Beta

Six months answering three questions in order: is the loop fun, can the destruction be synchronised over a network, and can bots carry the game when six people are not online. The end state is a Steam beta you can build a community around.

What it takes for you to be whole

At a seven dollar price point, after Steam's share and a realistic deduction for regional pricing and discounts, around 114,000 copies covers both our cash fee and the full recoup on the deferred portion. After that, fifteen percent of revenue comes to us and the rest is yours.

Put that against the first table. Content Warning has 163,000 reviews, and reviews typically represent somewhere between a thirtieth and a fiftieth of sales. So the plan does not require House Siege to become one of the genre's major titles. It requires the game to get finished and find a normal audience.

11Next steps

Your material runs three concepts in parallel. We suggest House Siege runs alone until the gate in step one is cleared. The other two lose nothing by waiting.

Because part of our fee sits on the back end, the agreement needs a clear counterparty and clear ownership. It is a formality, but it should be in place before we start.
