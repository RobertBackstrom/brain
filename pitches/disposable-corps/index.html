Disposable Corps

Rescope the loop, move to player-hosted servers, release in Early Access in month ten.

This is a high level plan to take Disposable Corps to a released Early Access in ten months. It sets out what is cut, what is kept, what it costs and how it is funded. The team and the monthly cost are fixed. Scope detail is confirmed in the first month, against the build.

Situation

Diagnosis

What a player meets today

The problems below come from the public demo, the playtest notes and the feedback already gathered on the publisher side. Most are readability and UX problems rather than content or production value problems.

The map is too large and reads as open ground
Only one sector of the front is live at a time. The rest is closed. The existing map is kept and gated per sector.
Players are unsure what they are supposed to do
One objective at a time, one visible front line in the HUD, one clock. The next thing to do is always on screen.
Bots kill the player too fast, so PvP feels unfair
Enemy AI accuracy is lowered and weighted toward suppression. Player deaths come mainly from other players. The player's own recruited squad is unchanged in role.
Squad AI pathing and giving orders both feel bad
Orders are reduced to three on a radial: advance to marker, hold, dig here. Pathing runs on a sector-bounded navmesh instead of the open field.
UI and UX are behind the rest of the game
A dedicated UI/UX designer from month two, owning HUD, onboarding and the order interface.
The whole thing is not yet fun for long enough
Every round builds toward one moment: the whistle, the climb over the top, the barrage.
Design

The rescoped core loop

The current build stacks four games: a shooter, a squad command layer, a base builder and a vehicle sim, spread across a map larger than any of them need. The problems above follow from that scope.

The plan cuts the game to one loop: you dig the trench, you fight in the trench you dug, and it is blown apart underneath you. Systems outside that loop are parked until after launch.

Phase 1 · 90 seconds
Dig in

The defender fortifies one bounded sector: fixed dig points, barricades, gun nests. The attacker picks an approach and support. Same structure every round.

Phase 2 · 5 to 7 minutes
Over the top

One objective: a single trench line, clearly marked, on a visible clock. Players recruit and command AI squads, which the game already supports, so a 5v5 fills a front rather than fielding ten soldiers.

Phase 3 · 60 seconds
The line moves

If the line falls, the front shifts to the next sector and the loop restarts with sides swapped. Three sectors is a match, twenty to twenty-five minutes. This is the shifting frontline described on the store page.

Low concurrency

A full front at twenty players online

Recruited AI squads already keep a match populated at low concurrent player counts. Retuning them protects that property rather than adding it. Match quality does not depend on a large online population.

Scope of work

Nothing here is new technology

Recruitment, squad orders, building, the economy and the phase cycle are all built. Sector gating, the order interface, HUD and AI tuning are design and integration work on those systems. No new core systems are built.

Scope

What waits until after launch

These systems are parked until after launch. Several are candidates for Early Access content drops.

In-match economy and factories Player-built tank production Nation rosters beyond two factions Ammo racks and track damage modelling Armour deformation thresholds Novelty structures PvE co-op as a marketed mode LAN as a selling point Tutorial as a separate level

The tutorial level is replaced by guidance inside the first round of a real match.

Infrastructure

Take the servers out of the cost base

The game already has a server list with a host region filter, and the store page already lists LAN play. The plan moves the game to a player-hosted listen server over Steam's relay network, keeping the browser and region filter that already exist. Dedicated servers become an option for communities rather than a fixed monthly cost per region.

Server cost then no longer scales with concurrency, and the game stays playable for as long as anyone hosts it. Confirming the exact route is the first technical task of the review phase.

Commercials

1 860 000 SEK cash for the full twelve months

The project costs 180 000 SEK a month across both sides. Aurora Punks caps its own cash at 1 500 000 SEK over the twelve month term and defers the rest of its fee, 300 000 SEK. The development team's 30 000 SEK a month is paid in cash. Total cash requirement is 155 000 SEK a month, 1 860 000 SEK over the term. The deferred amount is recouped at 2.5x on release, 750 000 SEK, after which Aurora Punks takes a 15 percent revenue share.

Working model

Roles and mandate

Aurora Punks takes the producer and product owner seat. In practice that means Aurora Punks raises the fixes, makes the case for why each one is needed until there is a shared view, and then the solution is agreed together. Where the development team wants to implement something itself, it does, against an agreed deadline.

The founders keep the IP, the vision and the game design, and keep writing the game. Aurora Punks owns scope, the release date and the product decisions that follow from them, and staffs the disciplines the project does not have.

Next

What we need to start

  • Repository and build access, plus engine and version
  • The playtest and wishlist data: retention, concurrency, funnel, where players dropped
  • A week with the team, remote is fine, to understand the codebase and how they work
  • An NDA directly with the developer. The existing one is with the publisher
  • A decision on the working model, or a green light for phase 0 while that is settled

Phase 0 can start within two weeks of a yes.
