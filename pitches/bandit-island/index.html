01The job

This is a port plus a feature build

We want to be straight with you about the shape of this before we talk about money, because it is the reason the number is bigger than a normal console port quote.

Moving the game to console is the small half. Rebuilding the input layer Amazon gives you for free is the large half.

The part that is a normal port

Platform bring-up on six SKUs, save data, achievements and trophies, store and entitlement hooks, suspend and resume, user and profile handling, memory and load-time work, and the technical requirement checklists for Sony, Microsoft and Nintendo. We do this work continuously and it is the predictable part of the estimate. On a quiz title with modest rendering demands it is not where the risk sits.

The part that is a feature build

On Luna your players scan a QR code and their phone becomes the controller, and they answer out loud. Neither of those is a thing the game does. They are things the Luna platform does, in Amazon's data centre, next to the machine your game is running on. On a console in someone's living room there is no data centre, no QR-join service and no speech recognition. All of it has to be built as part of the game, and it has to work on six different pieces of hardware with six different ideas about where a microphone lives.

We could quote you a cheap number by quietly dropping the phone and the voice and shipping a gamepad quiz. It would be roughly a third of the price. It would also not be the game you made, so we have costed the real one and put the cheaper path at the bottom of the budget section as a named alternative rather than hiding it in an assumption.

02The input layer

What Luna does for you, and how we replace it

You asked whether the Luna solution can be reused on console. The honest answer is that the Amazon implementation cannot come with you, but the shape of it can, and once we move one piece around it actually fits console better than it looks.

On Luna today

The game runs on Amazon hardware. The phone talks to Amazon's servers, which sit beside the running game, so pairing, input and speech recognition are all platform services and all of them are a short hop inside one data centre. Your game receives clean events and never handles a microphone.

On console, naively

The game runs on the box in the living room. There is no relay, no join service, no Alexa and no system speech recognition on any of the three platforms. Every service you are currently using disappears at once.

What we build

The console shows a room code. Players open a URL in their phone browser, no install and no pairing, exactly the model party games have shipped on every console for a decade, which is also why all three platform holders are comfortable with it. A small hosted relay pairs each phone to the console session and carries button presses, typed answers and wagers.

The move that matters

We put the speech recognition on the relay, not on the console. The phone captures the audio it already has the best microphone for and sends it to the relay, the relay returns text, and the console receives words and button events. It never sees audio and never loads a speech model. That single decision is what takes the memory problem off the Switch, and it gives us one identical code path on all six SKUs instead of six different microphone stories.

The three ways to do speech, and why we recommend this one
A

Recognition on the relay

Recommended. Audio goes phone to relay, text comes back. Identical on every platform. No speech model in the console's memory budget, which is the whole Switch argument. Costs: the game needs a network connection to play with voice, there is a small per-session running cost, and each platform will ask us a privacy question about audio leaving the device, which we answer once and reuse.

B

Recognition on the console

No network dependency and no running cost, and nothing leaves the living room, which is the easiest privacy story for a family title. It costs memory and CPU on the weakest box in the set. Viable because Jeopardy is not open dictation: at the moment a player buzzes we already know the correct response, so the job is scoring audio against a small candidate set rather than transcribing anything a person could say. That fits in tens of megabytes, not gigabytes. We would build this as a switchable second backend rather than the primary.

C

No voice, gamepad only

Cheapest by a wide margin and a genuinely different product. Answers become multiple choice or an on-screen keyboard, the buzzer becomes a face button, and the game stops being the thing people gather around the TV for. Costed at the bottom of the budget section so you have the number if Amazon needs a floor.

We would settle this in December, before the main port starts, because it decides the shape of everything after it. The output of that first gate is a written architecture decision, not a build.

Read the table top to bottom and the design falls out of it. For four people to answer out loud in the same room, on all six SKUs, the only microphone that is reliably present is the one already in their pocket. Build the phone path properly and every platform works the same way, including the one with no microphone at all.

On old-gen: a quiz title is exactly the kind of game that still sells on PS4 and Xbox One, and the incremental cost of both is small because they ride on the next-gen work. They are in the number above. If you would rather launch next-gen only and add them later, that removes roughly two months of the porting engineer and we can split it out.

Why UI and UX are full roles here

The phone screen is a second game surface with its own layout, its own input model and its own failure states, and it has to work in mobile Safari and Chrome across every handset size in a living room. Buzzer, answer entry, wager entry, the board, rejoining after someone's phone locks or drops off the wifi. That is a product, not a screen, and understaffing it is the most common way this kind of project goes wrong.

The second title, and the cheaper path

The second quiz title

Roughly a third of the first, in the order of 1,1 MSEK and three to four months, if it runs on the same engine and the same codebase and we do it while the team is still together. The relay, the companion client, the platform integration and the six-SKU bring-up all exist by then. What is left is the game itself, a UI skin and a second trip through certification. That number is a rough indication rather than an estimate, because we have not seen the title. The useful point is that the expensive part is expensive once.

Gamepad only, no phone and no voice

In the order of 1,1 MSEK and five to six months. Answers become multiple choice or an on-screen keyboard, the buzzer becomes a face button, and everything in section 02 about the input layer goes away. It is a conventional console port and priced like one. We are not recommending it, because it removes the reason the game exists on Luna, but if there is a budget ceiling that decides this then you should have the number rather than us pretending it does not exist.

07Open items

What this estimate is standing on

We have not read the code yet. These are the assumptions the number rests on, and the ones we would most like to close before it hardens into a quote.

Source access
The largest single thing between this estimate and a firm quote. Two weeks with the code turns most of what follows from a range into a number, and historically our estimates move by more than twenty per cent once we can see how the input and UI layers are actually structured. We understand this is waiting on Amazon approval tiers and we are not pushing. It is just worth saying plainly that the estimate is worth more once it happens.
Engine and architecture
We have assumed a single engine across both titles and a codebase where the Luna input layer is reasonably separable from the game logic. If input handling is woven through the game code, the port side grows. This is the first thing we would look at.
How coupled the game is to Luna services
Beyond input and voice, anything using Amazon identity, entitlement, session or storage services needs a console equivalent. We have priced the obvious ones. We cannot price the ones we cannot see yet.
Switch memory
4 GB shared, of which a game gets roughly 3,2 GB. Moving speech recognition off the device removes the part of this we were most worried about. What is left is a normal addressables and texture-budget pass, which is routine work and is in the estimate. We would measure it against the real project at Gate 1 rather than guess now.
Rights for console distribution
Not our work, but it sits on our critical path. If the Jeopardy licence is scoped to Luna, extending it to three console platforms is a conversation with a lead time, and it is cheaper to start it in parallel with Gate 1 than to discover it at submission.
Who publishes, and who holds the accounts
Whether the titles ship under Amazon's platform accounts or Bandit Island's changes who submits, who is liable at cert and who owns the store pages. It does not change the engineering, but it changes the release plan and it should be settled before Gate 5, not during it.
Certification windows
Three platform holders, six SKUs, and a failed submission costs weeks rather than days. September and October are budgeted for that rather than as a formality, and the team is deliberately still staffed through it.
08Next step

What we would like to do next

Source access, and two weeks with it. We come back with a firm quote instead of an estimate, and with the architecture question in section 02 answered against your actual code rather than against what we could see from the outside.

If Amazon's approvals run longer than that, the December start still holds. Gate 1 is a design decision and a technical document, and most of it can be done on the Luna build and a conversation with your engineers. We would rather start the thinking in December and correct it in January than lose the window entirely.
